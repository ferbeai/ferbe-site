AI Governance

You use AI. Your ISMS doesn’t know.

Almost every ISO 27001 template pack is silent on artificial intelligence. That silence is not neutral — it is a documented gap sitting in your risk assessment, waiting for an auditor to find.

FL
Falco Lee
Founder, Ferbe
·28 July 2026·7 min read

Try this before you read further

Open whichever ISO 27001 template pack your organisation is working from. Search it for “AI”.

In most cases nothing comes back. Not in the acceptable use policy, not in the supplier assessment, not in the risk register, not in the Statement of Applicability. The word simply is not there.

Now ask a second question: does anyone in your organisation use AI? Copilot in Microsoft 365. ChatGPT for drafting an email. An AI feature your CRM vendor switched on last quarter. Someone in engineering using a coding assistant.

If the answer to the second question is yes and the answer to the first is nothing, you have a gap. Not a missing nice-to-have — a gap.

Why the silence exists

It is worth being fair about the reason, because it is not laziness.

AI governance sits awkwardly between standards. ISO 27001:2022 Annex A contains no control that names artificial intelligence — Annex A was finalised before generative AI became a routine part of office work. ISO/IEC 42001 exists as a dedicated AI Management System standard, but it is a separate certification with its own audit cycle, and most organisations that simply use AI do not need it.

So template providers and consultants face a genuine question: where does AI belong? The path of least resistance is to leave it out and wait for the standards bodies to catch up.

That is understandable. It is also not defensible at a Stage 2 audit.

Clause 6.1.2 does not care which standard has the control number

The requirement is simple. You must identify risks to information within your ISMS scope.

When an employee pastes a customer contract into a public AI tool to summarise it, information within your scope has left your control. It has been transmitted to a third party, processed under terms most people have not read, and possibly retained. That is a data egress event.

Whether Annex A has a numbered control for it is beside the point. The risk exists. If your risk assessment does not mention it, your risk assessment is incomplete — and incompleteness in the risk assessment is a finding, because Clause 6.1.2 is a mandatory clause rather than an optional Annex A control.

This is the part organisations tend to miss. They think of AI governance as an advanced topic for AI companies. It is not. It is a basic scoping question for anyone whose staff have access to a browser.

What an auditor actually asks

Certification bodies have adjusted faster than template providers. The questions now surfacing at Stage 2:

  • Does your organisation use AI tools? Which ones?
  • Where does that appear in your risk assessment?
  • What does your acceptable use policy say about AI?
  • Have you assessed your AI vendors as suppliers?
  • Who is accountable when an AI-informed decision goes wrong?

None of these are unreasonable. All of them are difficult to answer from a template pack that never mentions AI.

The controls you already have, applied properly

The encouraging part is that you mostly do not need new controls. You need existing controls to acknowledge that AI exists.

ControlWhat changes when AI is in scope
A.5.10Acceptable use must state which AI tools are approved and what data must never be entered into any of them
A.6.3Awareness training should cover prompt injection, data leakage through prompts, and treating AI output as unverified
A.5.19–A.5.21AI vendors are suppliers. Contracts raise data residency, training-data use, and opt-out questions that ordinary SaaS agreements do not
A.8.12Data loss prevention should account for personal data being submitted to unauthorised AI services
A.8.23Web filtering can enforce your approved AI tool list at the network layer
A.5.34Personal data processed through AI needs a Data Protection Impact Assessment

Add a short AI acceptable use policy, a register of tools actually in use, and a named owner, and most organisations have closed the gap.

Shadow AI is the harder problem

Shadow IT took years to become a recognised risk category. Shadow AI arrived in months, and it is harder to detect because it leaves less trace. There is no software to install and no procurement request. Someone opens a browser tab.

The practical consequence is that your AI tool register is almost certainly incomplete, because the tools that pose the most risk are the ones nobody declared. This is why the acceptable use policy matters more than the technical control: you are trying to change behaviour among people who do not think of what they are doing as a security decision.

Where regional regulation is heading

The regulatory picture varies by jurisdiction, and it is moving. In Malaysia, MDEC AI Ethics Principles and the National AI Roadmap set expectations for responsible AI use. Singapore has the IMDA Model AI Governance Framework, with MAS FEAT Principles applying to financial services. The EU AI Act creates binding obligations including risk classification and conformity assessment for high-risk systems. The US has the NIST AI Risk Management Framework; Australia has its AI Ethics Principles and Voluntary AI Safety Standard.

None of these replace ISO 27001. All of them are easier to approach if your ISMS already accounts for AI rather than treating it as a separate future project.

A reasonable position

You do not need ISO 42001 certification to use Copilot responsibly. You do not need a dedicated AI governance function. What you need is for your existing ISMS to reflect reality.

If AI touches information within your scope, it belongs in your risk assessment — regardless of which standard has a control number for it.

That is a defensible position at audit, and it is achievable in a few documents rather than a separate certification programme.
